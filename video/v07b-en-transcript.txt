The Wronskian and the fundamental set of solutions

[0:00] To build a general solution from two solutions, they must be linearly independent. A convenient test is the Wronskian determinant. But it must be used with care: the conclusions differ for arbitrary functions and for solutions of an equation.

[0:17] For two functions the Wronskian is y one times y two prime minus y one prime times y two. If the functions are dependent on an interval, the Wronskian vanishes identically there. So if the Wronskian is nonzero at even one point, the functions are certainly independent. For example, for e to the x, e to the two x and e to the three x the Wronskian is two e to the six x.

[0:45] Now a cautionary example: x squared and x times the absolute value of x, on the interval from minus one to one. For positive x both functions equal x squared; for negative x the second equals minus x squared. In both cases the Wronskian is zero, so it vanishes identically.

[1:07] But are they dependent? Suppose alpha one x squared plus alpha two x times absolute x is identically zero. At x equals one half, alpha one plus alpha two is zero; at x equals minus one half, alpha one minus alpha two is zero. So both alphas are zero and the functions are independent! Conclusion: for arbitrary functions a zero Wronskian does not imply dependence.

[1:38] For solutions the situation is different. If y one and y two solve one linear homogeneous equation with continuous coefficients, the Wronskian is either zero everywhere or nowhere. The Abel–Liouville formula shows it: W equals W at the starting point times e to the minus integral of p, and an exponential is never zero.

[2:04] The graph shows x squared and x times absolute x. On the right they coincide, on the left they are mirror images. No single constant factor works on both sides, so the functions are independent.

[2:19] n independent solutions of an n-th order equation form a fundamental set. Every solution of the homogeneous equation is their linear combination. The general solution of the nonhomogeneous equation is that combination plus one particular solution. For y double prime plus y equals x it is C one cos x plus C two sin x plus x.
