Heun, RK4 and stability

[0:00] Euler’s method is simple but not very accurate. In this part we look at methods that sample the slope at several points and average: the improved Euler or Heun method, and the classical Runge–Kutta method. Then we see why a large step can be dangerous.

[0:19] In Heun’s method we first predict with Euler, then compute the slope at the end of the step and step with the average of the two slopes. RK4 takes four slopes: at the start, twice at the midpoint and at the end, and averages them with weights one, two, two, one.

[0:39] Again y prime equals x plus y, y of zero equals one, h equals zero point one. In RK4, k one equals one. k two is at the midpoint: x is zero point zero five and y is one point zero five, so k two equals one point one.

[1:00] k three is also at the midpoint but uses k two: one point one zero five. k four is at the end of the step: one point two one zero five. Averaging gives y one about one point one one zero three four two, with an error of about two ten-millionths.

[1:20] Compare the three methods at x equal to zero point five. When the step is halved the Euler error drops by two, Heun’s by four and RK4’s by sixteen. So the orders are one, two and four.

[1:35] Now stability. For y prime equals minus lambda y, Euler multiplies y by one minus h lambda at every step. If h is larger than two over lambda, this factor exceeds one in size, and the numerical solution grows with alternating sign, although the exact solution on the graph decays.
