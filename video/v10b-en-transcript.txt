The Abel–Liouville formula

[0:00] Variation of parameters needs two homogeneous solutions. For variable coefficients they are hard to find. But if one is known, the second can be obtained from a formula. The key is the Ostrogradsky and Liouville formula, also called Abel’s formula.

[0:19] Differentiate the Wronskian: W prime equals y one times y two double prime minus y one double prime times y two. Substituting the second derivatives from the equation gives W prime equals minus p times W. This is a first-order linear equation, solved by W equals W zero times e to the minus integral of p.

[0:43] An important consequence: for solutions of one equation the Wronskian is either identically zero or never zero. But this is false for arbitrary functions: x squared and x times the absolute value of x have zero Wronskian yet are linearly independent. They cannot be solutions of such an equation.

[1:04] The second-solution formula: y two equals y one times the integral of e to the minus integral of p, over y one squared. Example: x squared y double prime minus three x y prime plus four y equals zero, x positive, with y one equal to x squared known.

[1:26] First normalise: dividing by x squared gives p equal to minus three over x. Minus the integral of p is three log x, and its exponential is x cubed. Under the integral we get x cubed over x to the fourth, which is one over x. Its integral is log x.

[1:47] So y two equals x squared log x, and the general solution is C one x squared plus C two x squared log x. Check: substituting the derivatives of y two, the x squared log x terms and the remaining terms cancel separately, giving zero.

[2:07] The graph shows two solutions: x squared and x squared log x. At x equal to one the second one vanishes, but they are nowhere proportional. Their Wronskian is x cubed, non-zero for positive x, in agreement with Abel’s formula.
